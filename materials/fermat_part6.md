It comes down to one thing: integers are far more rigid than real or complex numbers, and that rigidity is invisible to anything except deep arithmetic.

If you allow x, y, z to be real numbers, x³+y³=z³ has a whole continuous surface of solutions — trivially easy, no theorem needed. The demand that x, y, z be exact integers is what turns a two-line equation into something that took 358 years, because integrality isn't a property calculus or continuous analysis can see at all. That's exactly what killed several of the "obvious" attacks in this investigation — anything built on continuous/extremal reasoning (calculus-style arguments) gets zero traction on a Diophantine equation, because it can't distinguish an integer point from any other point nearby.

The cleanest actual explanation, though, is geometric — the genus of the curve. Rewrite x^n+y^n=z^n as a curve in the plane (dividing through by z^n): x^n+y^n=1. The genus of a smooth curve of degree n is (n−1)(n−2)/2. That single number governs everything:

- **n=2:** genus 0. A conic. Genus-0 curves with a rational point admit a full rational parametrization — this is exactly Euclid's formula for Pythagorean triples, and it's why n=2 has infinitely many solutions and is completely easy.
- **n=3:** genus 1. The Fermat cubic is literally an elliptic curve. Genus-1 curves are richer than genus-0 but still classically tractable by descent — which is exactly why Euler's method (exploration 2) worked at all, using only 18th-century tools.
- **n≥4:** genus ≥3 (genus 3 at n=4, growing quadratically after that) — "general type" curves. A deep 20th-century theorem (Faltings, 1983, proving the Mordell Conjecture) says such curves have only finitely many rational points. That's already one of the harder theorems in arithmetic geometry, and it only gives finiteness — not zero. Getting all the way to zero needed something else entirely.

So the genus jumps from 0 to 1 to ≥3 in the first three cases, and each jump crosses into a genuinely different regime of mathematics — parametrizable, classically-descendable, then "you need serious 20th-century machinery just to know the solution set is finite." That's the real reason the difficulty explodes even though the exponent just ticks up by one at a time.

And the actual proof doesn't even attack the high-genus Fermat curve directly — Wiles' strategy (after reducing to prime exponents, since n=4 was already done and every other n has a prime factor) takes a hypothetical counterexample and builds an auxiliary elliptic curve from it (the Frey curve), then shows that curve can't exist because it would violate the modularity of elliptic curves. So the proof routes back down to genus 1 — the one case classically tractable — but via a completely different curve than the one the equation naturally suggests, tied to the original equation only through a clever indirect construction. That's the "unexpected connection between two fields" from a few messages ago, in concrete geometric terms: elliptic curves (genus-1 geometry) and modular forms (analysis on the upper half-plane) turn out to organize every elliptic curve, and that fact — proved for exactly the curves needed — is what finally closes a genus-≥3 problem indirectly.

That's the honest shape of "why": short statement, but the object it's actually about (a high-genus algebraic curve, demanding integer, not just rational, points) sits at a level of the mathematical hierarchy where nothing simple was ever going to reach it — and every explored classical near-miss in this investigation failed at exactly the boundary where that hierarchy starts demanding more.
